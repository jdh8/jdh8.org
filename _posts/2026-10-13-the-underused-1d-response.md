Recently, I have been working on 5-card major bidding systems:

- [Muscat Walsh](https://jdh8.github.io/muscat-walsh/)
- [Watermelon Dutch Doubleton](https://jdh8.github.io/watermelon-dutch/)
- [Strawberry Polish Club](https://polish.club)

These systems somehow work on the same problem.  As [Richard Granville][ric]
puts it, "the 1♦ response to 1♣ is severely underused."  Each system hands 1♦ a
new job, and the job decides how strong 1♣ can be.

[ric]: https://bridgewinners.com/article/view/is-fantunes-still-a-good-bidding-system/

![Bar chart of the strength of 1♣: Muscat Walsh 11--20, Watermelon Dutch
Doubleton 11--23, Strawberry Polish Club 11 with no upper
limit]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/1c-ceiling.svg)

Transfer Walsh rearranges responses to a natural 1♣---1♦ for hearts, 1♥ for
spades, and 1♠ for hands without a 4-card major.  The 1♠ response needs some
points (about 4+) so that an 18--20 opener can bid beyond 1NT and land on 2NT.
The ceiling of 1♣ is roughly the same as in plain 2/1 because we still pass
3-counts, especially those without a 4-card major.

Polish Club takes the most radical way.  The 1♣ opening is home to all strong
hands, where 1♣ - 1♦ serves as a negative relay.  The 2♣ opening is freed up for
preemptive uses.

Dutch Doubleton is a lawyer's Polish Club with a quasi-natural 1♣.  Since 1♣
can hold 21--23, responder passes only a 0--5 hand with 4--5 clubs, a hand that
cannot survive 1NT anyway.  Every other weak hand goes through 1♣ - 1♦, a
negative relay to park it.  Otherwise a 3-count would respond 1♠ and push an
18--20 opener past 1NT.

The real lever is how often responder passes.  The fewer hands pass 1♣, the more
1♣ can hold, and vice versa.  Walsh still passes weak hands, so 1♣ keeps a
natural-ish ceiling.  Dutch Doubleton passes only a sliver of hands, with 1♦ as
the parking spot for the rest, so 1♣ reaches 23.  Polish Club never passes 1♣,
so 1♣ has no ceiling at all and contains all strong hands.
